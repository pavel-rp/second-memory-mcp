# Learning-Science Evidence Audit — Spaced-Repetition Teaching System

**Scope**: audit of the scheduler (modified SM-2), retrievability-tiered teaching loop, LLM-graded quality signal, sequencing/prerequisite logic, and analytics of the system implemented in `src/domain/algorithms/`, `src/orchestration/teaching-workflows.ts`, and related modules, against the empirical learning-science and SRS-algorithm literature.

**Method**: 14 questions were adjudicated against live-verified sources (WebFetch/WebSearch performed in this session; every citation below carries a URL and a verbatim quote or exact datapoint pulled from that source). Claims recalled from training data that could not be confirmed against a live source in this session are explicitly marked **UNVERIFIED**. Three implementation claims from the recon brief (dead `leechFailureThreshold=6`, dead `interleaveStrategy`/session-composition config, absence of fuzz/jitter) were independently spot-checked against the actual source tree via `Grep` and confirmed exactly as described before researching literature against them. Research for Q2/Q3/Q4/Q6/Q7/Q8/Q9/Q10/Q11/Q12/Q13 was parallelized across four sub-agents, each instructed to attach a URL and a verbatim datapoint to every citation and to mark unconfirmed recollections as UNVERIFIED; the load-bearing anchor citations (FSRS-vs-SM-2 benchmark, Anki leech/fuzz/stats docs, Rawson & Dunlosky, Dunlosky et al. 2013 utility tiers, Cepeda et al. scope) were independently re-verified by the lead researcher via direct fetch. The three most recent/least-common arXiv IDs cited in Q3–Q4 (2509.16533, 2605.16207, 2604.16790) were independently re-fetched at the end of this audit and confirmed to resolve to real papers matching the claimed titles and topics exactly.

## 1. Executive Summary

**Letter grade: C+ (functional and directionally reasonable in places, but several core mechanisms are contradicted by current best practice, and the system has no way to detect whether it is actually working).**

Rationale for the grade: of the 14 questions adjudicated, 2 are flatly **Contradicted** by the reference literature/deployed systems (prerequisite-mastery threshold, full-reset lapse handling), most of the rest are **Partially supported** (directionally reasonable ideas implemented in an evidentially loose way — the FSRS-tier grafting, the roadblock gate, the leech threshold, the retrievability-tiered instruction, interleaving's absence), one core mechanism (LLM self-grading) has a well-evidenced reliability problem with only a narrow mitigation in place, and the measurement layer is **Supported** as a real gap — the system collects rich attempt-level data but computes none of the standard SRS health metrics from it, and the two ungrounded numeric defaults (90-minute session cutoff, 200 review/day cap) have **no evidence either way**. This is not a system built on faulty premises — many pieces (retry-with-hint, retrievability-tiered instruction, leech flagging) trace to real ideas in the literature — but several load-bearing thresholds and reset rules diverge from what deployed reference systems and controlled studies actually recommend, and there is no closed loop to catch that divergence.

**The 3 most consequential findings:**

1. **Prerequisite "mastery" = a single successful review, ever (`repetitions > 0`) — Contradicted by every reference mastery system checked.** Bloom's mastery learning uses ~90% on a formative test; Khan Academy's Bayesian mastery model and reported "Proficient" bar are far more stringent; Bayesian Knowledge Tracing conventionally advances only once P(mastery) crosses 0.95. A single lucky guess unlocks dependent content here. This is also the **cheapest fix in the entire audit** — the system already computes a retrievability estimate for teaching-tier selection; gating prerequisite advancement on that same value costs nothing architecturally. See Q9.
2. **Lapse handling fully resets progress (interval → 1 day, repetitions → 0) regardless of review history — Contradicted by FSRS's own post-lapse formula and by classical relearning/savings evidence.** FSRS's stability-after-lapse is explicitly a function of *prior* stability (capped below it, never zeroed); Ebbinghaus-savings research shows material "forgotten" by a recall test still relearns faster than fresh material. A chunk with 20 successful reviews and a chunk on its first review are treated identically the instant either lapses once. See Q6.
3. **No closed-loop self-validation, compounding an unmitigated grading-reliability risk.** The system collects `time_spent_ms` and full per-question attempt history but computes no true-retention rate, no calibration check, and no forgetting-curve fit against actual outcomes — there is no way to tell whether the scheduler's implicit assumptions match observed recall. This matters more than it would in a simpler system because grading itself is delegated to an LLM that judges its own generated question-answer interaction with no independent check: the literature shows LLM-judge consistency can swing ~36 points on incidental framing alone, and LLM tutors specifically over-validate incorrect answers in the hardest cases — exactly where grading matters most. The one guardrail in place (the session-scoped quality cap) only catches drift *after* an early low score already occurred in that session; a judge that is uniformly lenient throughout never trips it. See Q3, Q13.

## 2. Per-Question Verdicts

### Q1 — SM-2 vs. FSRS in 2026; is grafting FSRS retrievability onto SM-2 state sound?

**Verdict: Partially supported.**

- Expertium/open-spaced-repetition benchmark (primary source, live-fetched): dataset of **9,999 Anki collections, 349,923,850 reviews** after filtering. Verbatim: **"FSRS-6 (recency) has a 99.6% superiority over Anki SM-2"** (log loss) — i.e., for 99.6% of the 9,999 tested collections, FSRS-6 produced lower (better) log loss than SM-2. The same source explicitly cautions: **"There is no way to have a truly fair, no caveats, comparison between FSRS and SM-2"**, because SM-2 was never designed to output calibrated recall probabilities — the benchmark team built a conversion formula just to make the comparison possible at all. https://expertium.github.io/Benchmark.html
- The widely-repeated "FSRS needs 20–30% fewer reviews than SM-2 for equal retention" figure appears consistently across secondary sources (e.g. https://studyglen.com/guides/best-spaced-repetition-apps and numerous SEO-style blogs) but a primary open-spaced-repetition table with this exact percentage could not be pulled directly in this session (GitHub wiki pages returned navigation/redirect stubs to the fetch tool rather than rendered tables). **Treat the direction as well-supported by the log-loss result above; treat the specific "20–30%" number as secondary-sourced, not independently confirmed against a primary table.**
- Anki's own manual (live-fetched, https://docs.ankiweb.net/deck-options.html) presents FSRS only as "an alternative" to SM-2 with a qualitative claim ("it can help you remember more material in the same amount of time") and no percentage figure — Anki's own docs don't assert the 20–30% number either.
- FSRS deliberately uses a 4-point rating scale (Again/Hard/Good/Easy = 1–4), not SM-2's finer 6-point (0–5) scale: **"This is a UX win — most humans can't honestly self-rate on a 6-point scale, but they can pick which of four buttons feels right."** https://expertium.github.io/Algorithm.html — relevant to Q2 as well.

**Is the graft (using SM-2's interval as an FSRS-style stability proxy for tier selection) sound or self-inconsistent?** Theoretically inconsistent, practically low-stakes for its actual narrow use. FSRS's Stability (S) is *fitted* via optimization against a large observed review-outcome dataset — it represents an empirically-grounded days-to-90%-recall constant. SM-2's interval is an artifact of the EF heuristic, never calibrated against any observed recall outcome for that specific item/learner. Treating "SM-2 interval" as "FSRS stability" in `R = (1 + (19/81)·daysOverdue/interval)^-0.5` silently assumes the SM-2 interval already encodes a genuine 90%-recall time constant, which nothing in modified-SM-2's design guarantees. However, per the recon facts, this R value is used **only** to bucket into four ordinal instruction tiers (0.7/0.5/0.3 cutoffs) — not fed back into scheduling. A purely ordinal classifier only needs the formula to be *monotonic* (more-overdue-relative-to-its-own-schedule → lower R), which holds regardless of whether S is a true fitted stability or a rough proxy. **Verdict nuance: theoretically unsound as a literal retrievability estimate; practically defensible as a monotonic "how overdue is this, relative to its own history" signal for tier selection only.** The risk is if this R value is ever repurposed for anything more (surfaced to users as "your retention is X%," used to prioritize review order) — at that point the lack of rigor becomes consequential because it implies false precision.

**Migration path**, given stored state (EF, interval, reps, full attempt history in `session_question_attempts`):
1. Backfill: replay `session_question_attempts` into per-chunk review-log sequences (timestamp deltas + quality mapped onto FSRS's 1–4 scale, e.g. <3→Again, 3→Hard, 4→Good, 5→Easy).
2. Feed reconstructed logs into an open-source FSRS optimizer (`fsrs-optimizer`/`ts-fsrs`) to fit global or per-learner weights — collection size matters (FSRS optimizer typically wants several hundred+ reviews to fit stably; for a modest single-learner chunk count, start from FSRS's published default parameters until enough history accrues).
3. Warm-start each chunk's Stability/Difficulty from its own historical attempt sequence.
4. Replace the EF/interval heuristic with FSRS's next-interval-for-target-retention calculation; the existing tier-selection logic can largely stay, now consuming a genuinely fitted R instead of a proxy — a net simplification, since two parallel "retrievability-shaped" computations collapse into one real one.
5. Reconsider whether to preserve granular 0–5 quality input (mapped down to FSRS's 4-point scale) or move to native 4-point input, given FSRS's explicit design rationale that finer self-rating isn't reliable anyway.

### Q2 — Binary quality signal in assessment mode (pass→5, fail→1)

**Verdict: Partially supported.**

- SM-2 original spec (Wozniak, live-fetched): quality grades are **"5 – perfect response; 4 – correct response after a hesitation; 3 – correct response recalled with serious difficulty; 2 – incorrect response; where the correct one seemed easy to recall; 1 – incorrect response; the correct one remembered; 0 – complete blackout."** https://www.supermemo.com/en/archives1990-2015/english/ol/sm2 — the scale encodes *difficulty of successful recall*, not just correct/incorrect.
- The EF update formula (same source, verbatim): **`EF':=EF+(0.1-(5-q)*(0.08+(5-q)*0.02))`**, noting **"for q=4 the E-Factor does not change."** Applying this formula to each grade: q=5 → EF+0.10; q=4 → EF+0; q=3 → EF−0.14; q=2 → EF−0.32; q=1 → EF−0.54; q=0 → EF−0.80.
- **This is the concrete, derivable mechanism by which binary collapse corrupts EF trajectories**: forcing pass→5 doesn't just discard nuance — it makes *every* successful assessment answer register as the single best possible response (max EF increase), when in reality many correct-but-effortful answers should behave like q=3 (EF *shrinks* by 0.14, "barely held"). And fail→1 lands one step above the worst possible bucket, so a close-but-wrong answer is scheduled almost identically to a total blackout. Caveat: this is Wozniak's canonical polynomial; the audited system is a "modified SM-2" with its own simpler piecewise EF rule (+0.10/−0.02/−0.20), but the qualitative pattern — pass always reads as flawless, fail always reads as near-worst — holds for any monotonic EF-vs-quality mapping collapsed to two endpoints, and directly explains why q=3/q=4 (the "recalled with effort" signals) matter and are lost.
- FSRS, the current best-performing successor, explicitly did **not** go binary: its difficulty-update formula takes the raw grade G (1–4) directly: **`D′(D,G) = w7·D0(3) + (1-w7)·(D - w6·(G-3))`**. https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/awesome-fsrs/wiki/The-Algorithm — the field's current best algorithm treats "how well" as load-bearing for scheduling, not just "whether."
- Anki/SM-2-family community evidence that reduced-granularity input has a known failure mode: **"'Ease Hell' is when a card's ease factor keeps decreasing... Some users only use the Again and Good buttons (i.e., Pass/Fail)... In this case, the ease factor of a card will never increase."** https://kuroahna.github.io/anki_srs_kai/guide/issuesWithAnkiSM2.html — but the community is genuinely split: a separate widely-read guide argues **"For the vast majority of us the best option is to simply use Anki's 'Good' and 'Again' buttons"** https://tatsumoto.neocities.org/blog/how-to-review — so 2-grade *input* is a contested, not settled, design choice in the wild.
- UNVERIFIED: a claimed FSRS-community finding that "RMSE is lower for two-button users for almost any threshold" — only the research *proposal* could be found (https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/fsrs4anki/issues/498), with no confirmed published results. Do not cite as fact.

**Recommendation:** The forced pass=5/fail=1 mapping is the most defensible place to intervene, because it's not just "less granular" — it specifically overstates every success as flawless and every failure as near-blackout. If binary, single-attempt assessment is a deliberate product choice, keep it, but map pass→4 (EF-neutral) and fail→2 rather than 5/1, to stop mechanically inflating ease on every success and over-penalizing borderline failures. One-line constant change, no architecture cost.

### Q3 — LLM/agent self-graded quality reliability; is the session cap adequate mitigation?

**Verdict: Partially supported** (the reliability concern is well-evidenced; the session cap is a real but narrow mitigation, not a general fix).

**(a) LLM-as-judge / self-grading reliability:**
- Zheng et al., "Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena" (NeurIPS 2023): examined **"position, verbosity, and self-enhancement biases, as well as limited reasoning ability"**; even strong judges only **"achieve over 80% agreement, the same level of agreement between humans."** https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685 — best-case independent LLM judging matches *inter-human* agreement, not superhuman reliability, and known biases are baked in from the start.
- "Bias in the Loop: Auditing LLM-as-a-Judge for Software Engineering": judge consistency can be **"near-random 50.36"** without bias injection, while specific prompt framing **"inflates the Overall CR to roughly 86"** — a ~36-point swing from incidental framing alone. https://arxiv.org/html/2604.16790v1
- "Challenging the Evaluator: LLM Sycophancy Under User Rebuttal": conversational pushback (vs. side-by-side judging) raises persuasion to **56.1% vs 43.6%**; even when the LLM's original answer was correct, conversational rebuttal flipped it **45.2%** of the time vs 34.3% for judge-style evaluation; a casually assertive rebuttal achieved **84.5% persuasion but only 17.1% correction rate** (i.e. it flips right and wrong answers indiscriminately). https://arxiv.org/html/2509.16533 — directly analogous to a learner pushing back against a live judged score.
- "Confirming Correct, Missing the Rest: LLM Tutoring Agents Struggle Where Feedback Matters Most": F1 was **94–99%** for identifying optimal solutions but only **0–76%** for valid alternatives and **4–55%** for actually-incorrect solutions; some models **over-validated incorrect solutions up to 71% of the time**. https://arxiv.org/html/2605.16207 — the closest primary evidence to this system's exact mechanism, showing LLM tutors are reliable on the easy cases and unreliable exactly where grading matters most.
- UNVERIFIED: specific self-preference-bias percentages ("67–82%, 82% for GPT-4o") and a claimed "Gemini is systematically more lenient" finding — surfaced only via unfetched search summaries; not independently confirmed.

**(b) Human learner self-grading bias:**
- Anki community source directly on self-grading: **"Using the 'Easy' button can artificially inflate your card scheduling intervals and lead to forgetting and lower retention"**, hence the community's own risk-mitigation is to reduce grading granularity, not add nuance. https://tatsumoto.neocities.org/blog/how-to-review
- The "Ease Hell" phenomenon (above) is direct evidence that biased/restricted self-grading in an SM-2-family scheduler produces a known, named failure mode in the wild.
- Directly-fetched study on self-assessment miscalibration (105 preservice teachers): **"the bidirectional nature of the Dunning-Kruger effect... People who estimate that their performance will be the best perform worse, and vice versa,"** improving only after objective feedback. https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/en/works/lDxaLJMl/
- No controlled study specifically measuring self-grading accuracy *in flashcard/SRS systems* could be located and fetched in this session — the "learners inflate their own SRS grades" claim is community-consensus-supported, not empirically measured in a controlled study.

**Does the session cap address this?** Only partially. The cap (an early 0–1 score caps later same-chunk scores at 3; an early 2 caps at 4) fires only *after* a demonstrated stumble already occurred in-session. But the dominant failure modes documented above — baked-in verbosity/self-enhancement bias, sycophancy under rebuttal, systematic *over-validation* of incorrect answers — are not conditional on a prior low score. A judge that is uniformly lenient from the first turn (never emits an early low score) sails through the cap untouched. The cap targets a narrower problem (within-session drift after a stumble), not the literature's dominant failure mode (uniform leniency with no low-score anchor to trigger against).

**Recommendation:** Keep the cap (cheap, catches one real pattern), but don't present it as a general LLM-judge-reliability fix. Add: (1) an independent judge pass (separate from the question-generating LLM) for a sampled fraction of gradings, since self-grading-your-own-generated-interaction compounds self-preference bias; (2) anchor grading to objective signals where available (exact/fuzzy match against a canonical answer) rather than pure LLM judgment; (3) log quality-score distributions per session/model to detect uniform-leniency drift the cap structurally cannot see (this is also a Q13 measurement gap).

### Q4 — Two-attempt cap with pivot-hint retry; should failure trigger restudy?

**Verdict: Supported** (the 2-attempt structure with a hint retry) **/ Contradicted** (the absence of any restudy or correct-answer exposure after the second failure).

- Kornell, Hays & Bjork (2009), *J. Exp. Psych: LMC*: **"Unsuccessful retrieval attempts enhanced learning with both types of materials... taking challenging tests—instead of avoiding errors—may be one key to effective learning."** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19586265/ — directly supports giving a real second attempt (the pivot-hint retry) rather than immediately revealing the answer after one failure: the attempt itself is valuable, independent of success.
- Butler, Fazio & Marsh (2011), *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*, on the hypercorrection effect: **"The hypercorrection effect occurred on both the immediate and delayed final tests, but error correction decreased on the delayed test... high-confidence errors were more likely than low-confidence errors to be reproduced on the delayed test"** when the correction wasn't reinforced. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21989771/ — the key evidence against "just record the failure and move on": even effectively-corrected errors resurface later unless reinforced. (The hypercorrection effect itself originates with Butterfield & Metcalfe 2001 — that original paper was not independently fetched in this session; treat the origin attribution as secondary while the persistence findings above are directly confirmed.)
- Pashler, Cepeda, Wixted & Rohrer (2005), *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*: **"Supplying the correct answer after an incorrect response not only improved performance during the initial learning session—it also increased final retention by 494%."** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15641900/ — a very large, directly-confirmed effect specifically for corrective feedback *after an incorrect response* — exactly the moment (second failure) this system currently does nothing but record.
- Test-potentiated encoding study: final retention was Control (single study) 18%, Restudy-only 57%, Test-without-feedback 78%, **Test-with-performance-feedback 80%**. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10157468/ — the retrieval attempt carries most of the benefit, but some correct-answer exposure after the attempt produces the highest number — supporting a light-touch "show the answer" step rather than either silence or a heavyweight forced-restudy detour.
- Frontiers (2023): a study-study-test condition reached **~60%** delayed recall vs. **~50%** for all-study or all-test alone; **"balancing testing with repetition may allow for more information to be learned and retained."** https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/cognition/articles/10.3389/fcogn.2023.1258955/full
- Relevant caveat (same tutoring-reliability paper cited in Q3): the pivot hint's value depends on the LLM correctly diagnosing *why* the first attempt failed — and that paper found LLM tutors specifically unreliable at exactly this diagnostic task, which may blunt the hint's real-world benefit even though the underlying two-attempt structure is well-supported.
- UNVERIFIED: a reportedly large-scale (164,532 students / 17M problems) CHI study on retry persistence in ITS — could not be fetched (403), numbers only appeared in unfetched search summaries; do not cite the figures. No study was found pinning down "2" specifically as an optimal attempt count — the literature supports the qualitative shape, not this exact number.

**Recommendation:** Keep the 2-attempt cap with pivot hint. Add a lightweight correct-answer exposure step after the second failure — not a forced full restudy, just surfacing the correct answer/explanation before scheduling the next review — since the Pashler (494% retention increase) and test-potentiated-encoding (78%→80%, restudy-alone only 57%) results both point at "silently record and move on" as the specific condition the evidence argues against. Small addition (one more response field/step), not an architecture change.

### Q5 — Roadblock gate vs. successive relearning (Rawson & Dunlosky)

**Verdict: Partially supported** (the concept of practice-to-criterion has real grounding; the implementation captures the *massed* variant the same literature shows is inferior, and risks inflating the quality signal fed to SM-2).

- This entire line of research sits atop the broader, exceptionally well-replicated spacing-effect literature: Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted & Rohrer (2006), *Psychological Bulletin* 132(3):354–380 — a meta-analysis of **317 experiments across 184 articles** on distributed practice. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719566/ (bibliographic/scope facts confirmed via PubMed listing; the specific optimal-ISI-ratio number from this paper was not independently pulled and is not asserted here).
- Rawson & Dunlosky's successive-relearning definition (live-fetched, 2022 *Current Directions* synthesis): **"Successive relearning (SR) involves practicing a task until it is performed correctly and then practicing it again until it is performed correctly during other spaced practice sessions."** https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/09637214221100484 — note "other spaced practice sessions": the criterion-repetitions are explicitly required across *separate, spaced* sessions, not repeated immediately within one sitting.
- Direct massed-vs-spaced comparison (same source): **"One-week retention was better when students had recalled items correctly one time in each of three spaced sessions than when they had correctly recalled each item three times during a single session: 68% vs. 26% retention respectively."** This is close to a direct experimental analogue of the roadblock gate: the gate requires N *additional, immediate, same-session* correct answers (massed) before progression — structurally the inferior condition in Rawson & Dunlosky's own comparison, for the same total count of successful recalls.
- Rawson & Dunlosky (2011) also found **"no benefit of requiring four correct trials per item compared to three"** in the initial-learning-session criterion, and typically used just **1** correct recall per session across subsequent spaced relearning sessions (multiple secondary syntheses, e.g. https://www.sec-ed.co.uk/content/best-practice/a-teacher-s-guide-to-retrieval-practice-successive-relearning). Also: **"subsequent spaced relearning can override the initial benefits of overlearning"** — extra same-session correct answers beyond criterion don't reliably buy anything if spaced relearning follows anyway.

**Are the follow-up counts {q0:3, q1:3, q2:2, q3:1, q4:1, q5:0} defensible?** No direct empirical citation supports these exact numbers. The q0→3 case loosely echoes Rawson & Dunlosky's "3 correct recalls" *initial-session* criterion in raw count, but that resemblance is coincidental — their 3-correct criterion was for the *first exposure* session before any spacing began, not a same-session recovery gate embedded in an already-scheduled item with its own EF/interval state. The graduated taper is a sensible engineering heuristic, not something the literature specifically parameterizes this way — **no evidence either way** on the exact numbers, stated honestly rather than forced into "Supported."

**Does massed same-session re-questioning add durable-memory value or only performance?** Primarily performance, per the 68%-vs-26% finding. This matters doubly here because the roadblock gate's massed successes feed directly into "chunk-level quality = rounded mean of each question's last attempt," which SM-2 uses to set the next interval — a learner who fails, then passes N massed follow-ups (short-term priming, not durable encoding), can produce a session-mean quality that overstates real mastery, causing SM-2 to schedule as if the item were more durably known than the evidence justifies.

**Recommendation:** Keep an immediate light restudy/correction step after failure (evidence-supported, see Q4), but stop treating immediate massed follow-up successes as equivalent evidence of learning for EF/scheduling purposes. Convert the roadblock gate into genuine successive relearning: require the "recovery" correct recall(s) to occur in the *next* scheduled session(s), not stacked into the same session, before restoring full scheduling confidence — and cap how much a single session's massed performance can move EF/interval regardless of how many in-session follow-ups were required.

### Q6 — Lapse handling: full reset vs. FSRS-style partial retention

**Verdict: Contradicted.**

- FSRS post-lapse stability formula (live-fetched): **`S'_f(D,S,R) = w11 · D^(-w12) · ((S+1)^(w13) - 1) · e^(w14·(1-R))`**. https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/awesome-fsrs/wiki/The-Algorithm — post-lapse stability is a function of *prior* stability S, not a fixed constant: explicit partial retention, not a reset.
- **"min(…, S) is necessary to ensure that post-lapse stability can never be greater than stability before the lapse."** https://expertium.github.io/Algorithm.html — FSRS caps post-lapse stability *below* pre-lapse stability, but never zeroes it.
- Anki's production Rust FSRS implementation shipped a fix specifically to enforce this cap: PR **"Update to FSRS-rs v1.4.4 (clipping post-lapse stability)"** — https://github.com/ankitects/anki/pull/3628 (title/existence confirmed live).
- Real-world bug report: a user-reported post-lapse stability of 73 days was flagged as excessive, citing SuperMemo guidance that **"post-lapse interval usually oscillates in the range of 1-4 days for the default forgetting index of 10%."** https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/fsrs4anki/issues/246 — even the community consensus ceiling is "1–4 days," not zero/full-reset, and this is a *ceiling* concern (too long), not evidence that zero/1-day is the right floor either.
- Ebbinghaus savings method, modern replication: **"Savings is defined as the relative amount of time saved on the second learning trial as a result of having had the first,"** and material "completely forgotten" by recall tests still relearns faster than fresh material — Murre & Dros (2015), *Replication and Analysis of Ebbinghaus' Forgetting Curve*, PLOS ONE 10(7): e0120644, DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0120644.

**Precision note:** in this system, EF is *not* reset on lapse — it is penalized (−0.20, per the recon facts' standard quality<3 rule) and the penalized value is retained (floored at 1.3). What resets unconditionally is **repetitions → 0 and interval → 1 day**, regardless of how many successful reviews preceded the lapse. That is the specific point of contradiction: a chunk lapsing after 20 successful reviews and a chunk lapsing on its first-ever review land in an identical state (interval=1d, reps=0), which neither FSRS's stability-retention design nor the savings literature supports.

**Recommendation:** Replace the unconditional interval-to-1-day/reps-to-0 reset with a rule that scales the post-lapse interval by depth of prior learning (e.g., a fraction of the pre-lapse interval, with a floor above 1 day for well-established items) — mirroring FSRS's D/S-dependent post-lapse formula and the savings literature. EF's existing penalize-and-retain behavior does not need to change; the fix should target the interval/repetitions collapse specifically.

### Q7 — Leech threshold: 3 consecutive vs. Anki's 8 lifetime

**Verdict: Partially supported** (flag-and-intervene is well supported; the specific consecutive-3 threshold is likely more aggressive in practice than it looks next to Anki's 8).

- Anki manual (live-fetched, verbatim): **"Each time a review card 'lapses'... a counter increases. When this counter reaches 8, Anki tags the note as a leech and suspends the card."** https://docs.ankiweb.net/leeches.html
- Anki's counter is lifetime/cumulative, not consecutive (community-corroborated across multiple threads, not one crisp official quote): https://forums.ankiweb.net/t/reset-repetition-and-lapse-count/25154 and https://forums.ankiweb.net/t/reset-leech-count-after-a-long-period-of-non-use-of-anki/41109 — Anki's "8" can take years and many intervening successes to accumulate, whereas 3 *consecutive* failures can trigger almost immediately after a chunk is introduced. **This is not a comparable basis** — 3-consecutive is structurally far more sensitive than "8-lifetime" despite the smaller number.
- Community sentiment on lowering thresholds is mixed: some long-term users use 4; others argue lower thresholds generate even more leeches long-term. https://forums.ankiweb.net/t/alternative-leech-threshold-ease-instead-of-lapses/2540
- SuperMemo/Wozniak on leech intervention (verbatim): a leech is **"an element that causes problems in the learning process"**; recommended actions: **"Reedit most intractable items with a view to applying appropriate mnemonic techniques," "Reset elements... or dismiss them,"** or **"Simply delete them."** http://super-memory.com/archive/help99/leech.htm — Anki converges on the same philosophy: **"The most efficient method to deal with leeches is to change how the information is presented."** https://docs.ankiweb.net/leeches.html
- No academic ITS/knowledge-tracing literature was found using the term "leech" specifically (UNVERIFIED that any does) — the closest analog is generic "targeted remediation" in knowledge-tracing systems, directionally consistent with this system's remediation-flag behavior but not a term-for-term match.

**Recommendation:** Either raise the consecutive threshold (4 is a documented community middle-ground) or require a minimum number of total attempts before a consecutive-3 flag can fire, to avoid over-flagging chunks that are simply new/hard early on rather than genuinely intractable. Both Anki and SuperMemo treat leech-flagging as the *start* of a reformulate-or-delete workflow, not a terminal state — confirm the "human manually resolves it" step actually supports reformulation, not just exclusion. The dead `leechFailureThreshold: 6` field could be repurposed as an Anki-style lifetime-count fallback trigger running alongside the consecutive-3 trigger, rather than left inert.

### Q8 — No fuzz/jitter

**Verdict: Partially supported** (fuzz solves a real, documented problem; the problem is not exclusively a large-collection concern, and its absence is plausibly *more* proportionally visible in a small chunk pool).

- Anki manual (verbatim): **"Anki also applies a small amount of random 'fuzz' to prevent cards that were introduced at the same time and given the same ratings from sticking together and always coming up for review on the same day,"** and **"it is not possible to turn this feature off."** https://docs.ankiweb.net/studying.html
- Even with fuzz on, clumping still occurs at Anki scale: the "Anki Load Balancer" add-on exists to combat "Mega Review Days," reorganizing "mature cards across future sessions so that they don't all pile up at one time." https://willpeachmd.com/anki-load-balancer — evidence base fuzz alone is insufficient even at typical (often large) Anki scale.
- FSRS4Anki-helper ships further countermeasures beyond core fuzz: "Load Balance" (interval "adjusted by a random amount to make the distribution of reviews over time more uniform") and "Disperse Siblings" (spreading same-note cards apart to prevent interference). https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/fsrs4anki-helper
- Forum framing of fuzz mostly emphasizes large collections, though some small-scale users actively want to reduce/disable it. https://forums.ankiweb.net/t/adjusting-fuzz-factor/56902

**Reasoned inference (not a citation):** the mechanism fuzz addresses — same-day-introduced, same-performance items permanently co-scheduling — is triggered by batched lesson delivery (multiple chunks taught together with similar trajectories), not by total collection size. With no fuzz, a batch of N chunks taught together will co-land on day 1, day 7, day 7×EF, day 7×EF² forever, deterministically. In a small pool (tens to low hundreds of chunks) that clump is a much larger *fraction* of daily workload than the same clump would be in a 10,000-card Anki collection.

**Recommendation:** Add a modest randomized fuzz window on interval calculation (Anki's model — a few-percent randomization scaled to interval length). This system's batch-teaching pattern is exactly the scenario fuzz was built to solve, and it is lower engineering cost than load-balancer-style add-ons.

### Q9 — Prerequisite mastery = `repetitions > 0`

**Verdict: Contradicted.**

- Bloom's mastery learning (live-fetched): **"Mastery learning maintains that students must achieve a level of mastery (e.g., 90% on a knowledge test) in prerequisite knowledge before moving forward."** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mastery_learning — corroborated by Sal Khan invoking Bloom directly: **"if the student's at 70 percent or 80 percent correct, they should have as many chances as necessary to get to 90 percent-plus correct."** https://www.cultofpedagogy.com/khan-mastery-learning/
- Khan Academy's front-end levels reportedly require 100% correct on an exercise to reach "Proficient" (search-derived; the page 403'd on direct fetch — not verbatim-confirmed). Khan Academy's engineering blog confirms a Bayesian-network probabilistic mastery model exists on the backend. https://blog.khanacademy.org/understanding-topic-mastery-with-bayesian-networks/ (fetched; specific probability threshold not rendered in the fetch).
- Bayesian Knowledge Tracing (Corbett & Anderson 1994): a 2025 EDM paper states **"the widely adopted threshold of 0.95 remains relevant"** for P(mastery) before advancing, finding **"higher thresholds, such as 0.98, yield additional benefits."** https://educationaldatamining.org/edm2025/proceedings/2025.EDM.short-papers.4/2025.EDM.short-papers.4.pdf (secondary confirmation of the 1994 convention, not a direct quote from the 1994 paper itself).

Against all of these, `repetitions > 0` is a single binary event with no probability estimate and no accuracy percentage — and, per the system's own design, is checked entirely independently of the power-law retrievability estimate the same codebase already computes for teaching-tier selection.

**Recommendation:** Gate "prerequisite mastered" on the same retrievability estimate already computed for teaching-tier selection (e.g., require R ≥ 0.7, the system's own "recall tier" cutoff) instead of `repetitions > 0`, or at minimum require ≥2–3 consecutive correct repetitions AND retrievability above a floor. This reuses existing computation at essentially zero architectural cost — the single cheapest, highest-leverage fix identified in this audit.

### Q10 — No interleaving, and is "easy-medium-hard" the right kind?

**Verdict: Partially supported** (no-interleaving is a real loss, but the dead config would not have delivered the evidenced benefit even if wired up — it targets the wrong axis).

- Rohrer & Taylor (2007) geometric-solids task: delayed-test advantage for interleaving reported as **d = 1.34**, per peer-reviewed secondary citation: **"at the retention test given one week later, the interleaved group outperformed the blocked group (d = 1.34)."** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10658001/ (secondary confirmation; the primary PDF would not render as text in this session).
- Brunmair & Richter (2019) meta-analysis of interleaved learning: overall medium positive effect, **Hedges' g = 0.42**. https://www.psychologie.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06020400/2019/Brunmair_Richter_in_press__2019_META-ANALYSIS_OF_INTERLEAVED_LEARNING.pdf
- Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan & Willingham (2013), *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 14(1):4–58, rated **interleaved practice as MODERATE utility** — not high (high utility = only practice testing and distributed practice) and not low (low utility = rereading, highlighting, summarization, keyword mnemonic, imagery). https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/journals/pspi/learning-techniques.html
- Kornell & Bjork (2008): interleaving painting-style exemplars beat blocking on a transfer/induction test, **"even though participants consistently believed that blocking... had been more helpful"** (search-synthesis; the primary PDF 403'd, not independently re-verified).
- Every framing of the evidence base found titles itself around **category/problem-type** mixing (Kang's review is literally "Interleaved Training and Category Learning"; Rohrer's papers mix "problem types"). **No research literature was found treating difficulty-level (easy/medium/hard) sequencing as the "interleaving" manipulation** — searches for "difficulty interleaving" / "mixed difficulty practice" returned either generic category-interleaving framing or an unrelated concept ("easy-to-hard" curriculum shaping, not mixing at all). The dead `interleaveStrategy: 'easy-medium-hard'` config names a difficulty-ordering scheme — a real conflation with the actual evidence base, which is about mixing unrelated topic categories mid-session, not ramping difficulty.
- UNVERIFIED (paywalled, search-synthesis only): Hwang et al. (2025), "Undesirable Difficulty of Interleaved Practice," reportedly finds low-achieving learners need initial blocked practice before interleaving — thematically consistent with Dunlosky et al.'s "moderate, not high" rating, but not independently confirmed here.
- On prerequisite constraints: no research was found that explicitly models interleaving under a prerequisite DAG — the literature (Kornell/Bjork, Rohrer) uses independent, order-agnostic item sets. A non-academic practitioner source (blog-level, not peer-reviewed) is directly on point: **"Reviews micro-interleave not only the problem types in the original lesson, but also the component (prerequisite) skills"** — https://justinmath.com (as reported via live fetch) — i.e., deployed systems interleave prerequisite *review* material into later sessions rather than interleaving during initial teaching of dependency-constrained content.

**Recommendation:** The dead `easy-medium-hard` config would not have delivered the evidenced interleaving benefit even if wired up. If interleaving is added, target the topic/category axis — mix distinct chunks/concepts within a review session (respecting the prerequisite DAG for first-exposure ordering, but mixing already-unlocked cross-topic review material into later sessions) rather than difficulty-ordering. The highest-leverage version: interleave prerequisite review re-injections (already partially implemented as "stale prerequisite re-injection") with other due reviews.

### Q11 — Retrievability-tiered instruction (recognition fallback at R<0.3)

**Verdict: Partially supported.**

Desirable-difficulties side:
- **"A desirable difficulty is a learning task that requires a considerable but desirable amount of effort, thereby improving long-term performance"** — but bounded: **"The task must be able to be accomplished. Too difficult a task may dissuade the learner and prevent full processing."** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desirable_difficulty
- Bjork's retrieval-strength mechanism (verbatim): **"the lower the retrieval strength at that moment, the greater the boost in storage strength (i.e., learning)."** https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2016/5/10-1 — the strongest argument *against* downgrading to recognition when R is low, since low-R retrieval attempts are where the biggest learning gains occur — *if* the retrieval succeeds.

Scaffolding/ZPD side:
- VanLehn (2011), via ERIC (verbatim): **"the effect size of human tutoring was much lower: d = 0.79... the effect size of intelligent tutoring systems was 0.76, so they are nearly as effective as human tutoring."** https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ946764 — supports adaptive/scaffolded ITS approaches generally, though not difficulty-format-adaptation specifically.

Middle ground (fetch-verified, the closest primary-literature analogue to this exact design question):
- Metcalfe's region of proximal learning: **"The region of proximal learning—where a small amount of time and effort should yield maximal learning gains—consists of items... perceived to be close to being learned but are not yet thought to have been mastered."** Mechanism: **"People should strongly prefer to choose items for study that are easy... but are not perceived to be mastered."** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2742428/ — this argues *for* R-dependent adaptation, provided the adapted format stays inside the "accomplishable, not yet mastered" band rather than jumping straight to trivial recognition.
- Challenge Point Framework (Guadagnoli & Lee; motor-learning literature, not directly educational — search-synthesis): **"learning is low when functional difficulty is too low or too high"** (inverted-U) — generalizes the same point: there is an optimal difficulty zone per learner-state, not a single hardest-possible target.

**Nuance on the actual tier structure:** recognition (multiple-choice) only fires at R<0.3 ("scaffold" tier); R 0.3–0.5 ("reteach") is still open-ended recall. This is not "downgrade to MC whenever retrieval gets hard" — it is reserved for the regime where free/cued recall would very likely fail outright, which is precisely where the desirable-difficulties literature's own caveat and Challenge Point/region-of-proximal-learning would also recommend stepping down. This directionally supports the design.

**The real weakness is threshold crudeness, not direction:** a binary R<0.3 → multiple-choice flip is a step function, not the smooth "optimal challenge point" the literature describes, and it skips a level (straight from cued_recall at R≥0.5 to full recognition at R<0.3, with no heavier-hinted-recall step in between). There is no mechanism to re-promote within the session if the learner clears the MC question trivially.

**Recommendation:** Keep the general shape (support scales down as estimated retrievability drops) but soften the step function — add a hinted-recall intermediate step between cued_recall and scaffold, and re-promote within the same session if the learner succeeds easily at the lower tier, so the system tracks the moving optimal-challenge-point rather than parking the learner in the low tier until the next full review cycle.

### Q12 — Session-length (~90 min) and daily cap (200 reviews/day)

**Verdict: No evidence either way** for the specific numbers. The general direction (cap continuous sessions, cap daily load) is consistent with fatigue/vigilance research, but neither number is anchored to any study of spaced-repetition or flashcard performance specifically — stated honestly rather than forcing a clean "Supported."

- Mackworth's classic vigilance study: **"The participants' event detection declined between 10 and 15 percent in the first 30 minutes and then continued to decline more gradually for the remaining 90 minutes"** of a 2-hour session. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vigilance_(psychology) — decrement is well underway by 90 minutes in this foundational study, not just beginning (though this is passive monitoring, not effortful retrieval practice — uncertain transfer).
- Ariga & Lleras (2011), *Cognition*: sporadic mental breaks during a task prevent vigilance decrement vs. no breaks (mechanism: "goal habituation"). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21211793/ — supports micro-breaks as mitigation; does not specify a duration threshold that would anchor "90 minutes."
- Frontiers in Cognition (2025), "The vigilance decrement: its first 75 years": decrement **"robust after the first half hour on the task"**; regular 5-min/30-sec interruptions **"markedly reduced performance decline"**; explicitly **no consensus on ideal session length or break frequency**. https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/cognition/articles/10.3389/fcogn.2025.1632885/full — a 2025 review of 75 years of research finds no established "optimal session length."
- Frontiers in Psychology (2025) classroom micro-breaks study: quiz performance declined from 81.3% to 55.6% (micro-break group) and 82.5% to 51.5% (traditional-break group) across a session; 90-second micro-breaks every 10 minutes outperformed a single break. https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1589411/full — real evidence that continuous engagement degrades over roughly an hour-plus, and that the evidence-backed intervention is *periodic micro-breaks*, not a single long-session cutoff — architecturally different from "stop entirely at 90 minutes."
- Anki manual: **"If you are consistently learning 20 new cards a day, you can expect your daily reviews to be roughly about 200 cards/day."** https://docs.ankiweb.net/deck-options.html — the only place "200" ties to Anki's own docs, but framed as an illustrative *consequence* of a habit, not a recommended ceiling.
- Ultradian-rhythm literature sometimes invoked to justify "90-minute" blocks reports **"cycles of approximately 90 to 100 minutes"** in EEG/mood/performance oscillation over 9 hours (Lavie, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7870505/) — a spontaneous physiological oscillation, not a study of optimal session-stopping points; the "90-minute focus block" framing popular in productivity writing is a secondary/blog-level extrapolation from this (UNVERIFIED as a research recommendation).

**Recommendation:** Stop presenting 90 minutes / 200 reviews as evidence-derived — they aren't, in either the vigilance-decrement literature or Anki/FSRS community norms. Make both thresholds configurable per-user, documented as engineering defaults. Since the strongest evidence supports periodic brief breaks over a hard cutoff, consider a break prompt every 20–30 minutes (matching where vigilance-decrement research shows decline becoming "robust") plus using the already-collected-but-unused `time_spent_ms`/quality trend within a session as a real fatigue signal (rising latency + falling quality), instead of a fixed clock.

### Q13 — Missing measurement: minimal self-validating analytics

**Verdict: Supported** (for "true retention" and "calibration" as established, named SRS health-metric concepts) — weak-to-no evidence that "time to relearn" specifically is a standardized named metric anywhere in the ecosystem, flagged honestly rather than asserted as standard.

- Anki manual, Statistics page (live-fetched): **"This table shows your retention across different cards and different time frames... If you are using FSRS, your true retention is expected to be close to your desired retention."** https://docs.ankiweb.net/stats.html — confirms Anki ships a first-class "True Retention" table as a standard official health metric — exactly the "actual recall rate at scheduled review time" metric this system doesn't compute.
- Expertium's Blog: **"Desired retention is what you want, and true retention is what you get. The closer they are, the better."** https://expertium.github.io/Retention.html — the core self-validation check the FSRS community treats as standard.
- Expertium's Blog, Benchmark methodology: **"if an algorithm predicts an X% chance of something happening, it should happen X% of the time... That's good calibration"**; **"RMSE (bins) measures how well-calibrated an algorithm is,"** distinguished from **"AUC is not a measure of calibration but of discrimination."** https://expertium.github.io/Benchmark.html — calibration is a formally used, named evaluation axis in the FSRS ecosystem.
- GitHub wiki, open-spaced-repetition/awesome-fsrs, "The Metric": **"Log Loss tells us how 'surprised' the model should be when the real outcome shows up"** — directly measures calibration between predicted success probabilities and observed results; RMSE(bins) measures calibration "by grouping reviews by independent features: interval length, prior review count, and lapses." https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/awesome-fsrs/wiki/The-Metric — a concrete, implementable calibration methodology even without a full FSRS reimplementation.
- Anki manual confirms lapse *counting* is standard (**"when you click Again on a review card, it is called a lapse"**, https://docs.ankiweb.net/stats.html), but no evidence was found of "time to relearn" as a formally named standard metric in Anki/FSRS docs — sensible to add, but not verified as an ecosystem standard.

**Concrete minimal analytics to add**, each tied to what it newly lets the system detect:
1. **True retention rate** (overall + by tier/age band): % of due reviews scored as pass. Currently no pass/fail-at-due-time aggregation exists at all.
2. **Calibration check**: bucket reviews by implicit predicted-recall-probability and compare to observed pass rate (RMSE-bins style), or log-loss over reviews. Detects systematic miscalibration (e.g. real recall 70% vs. implicit 90% target) — currently zero mechanism to catch this.
3. **Forgetting-curve fit against actual outcomes**: fit observed pass-rate vs. elapsed-time-since-review and compare to the assumed decay shape.
4. **Lapse rate + time/reviews-to-relearn**: count Again-on-review events per period, plus reviews/time needed to restore a lapsed item to its prior interval band (a novel analogue of Anki's lapse tracking, not itself a verified standard term). Detects unstable items/decks that aggregate retention alone would mask.
5. **Actually use `time_spent_ms`**: a per-session latency-vs-quality trend gives a real, measured fatigue signal, replacing the unvalidated fixed 90-minute rule (Q12) with an evidence-based per-user stopping signal — nearly free, since the field is already collected.
6. **Resolve the dead cognitive-load module**: wire it to real signals (e.g. #5) or remove it — an uninvoked model is itself a measurement gap of the same kind as the missing retention metrics.

## 3. Ranked Top-5 Changes (by expected learning-outcome impact per engineering effort)

The three most consequential findings in the executive summary (Q9, Q6, Q13) are not automatically the highest-*leverage* changes — Q6 requires a genuine algorithm change (medium effort) even though its severity is high. Ranking strictly by impact-per-effort surfaces the cheap, high-value fixes first; Q9 and Q13 anchor both lists because they happen to be both severe *and* nearly free to fix.

1. **Q9 — Gate prerequisite "mastery" on the existing retrievability estimate instead of `repetitions > 0`.** Near-zero effort (the R value is already computed for tier selection; this only changes which comparison consumes it). High impact: premature unlocking is the one failure mode that corrupts everything downstream of it — a learner advances into dependent content before a prerequisite is genuinely known, and every subsequent recommendation compounds that error. Directly resolves a **Contradicted** verdict.

2. **Q13 — Add true-retention rate and a basic calibration check.** Low-to-medium effort (aggregation over already-logged attempt data; no new data collection required). High impact because it is the *enabling* change: without it, there is no way to verify whether any other fix in this list (or any future scheduler change) actually improved outcomes. Currently the system has zero closed-loop signal that its own assumptions match observed recall.

3. **Q8 — Add a modest randomized fuzz window to interval calculation.** Trivial effort (a small randomization term in one function). Real, if modest, impact: this system's batch-teaching pattern (multiple chunks introduced together, likely rated similarly) is exactly the scenario fuzz was built to solve, and the omission compounds workload spikes proportionally more in a small chunk pool than in a large Anki collection.

4. **Q2 — Fix the assessment-mode quality mapping (pass→4 instead of 5, fail→2 instead of 1).** Trivial effort (two constants). Removes a specific, mechanically-derivable distortion: every assessment pass currently registers as a "perfect, zero-hesitation" recall (max EF boost) and every fail registers one step above a total blackout, regardless of how close the answer actually was.

5. **Q4 — Add a lightweight correct-answer/explanation exposure step after the second failed attempt.** Low-to-medium effort (one additional response field/step in the teaching-workflow orchestration, no scheduling changes required). High expected impact per unit effort: this is backed by one of the largest, most directly-confirmed effect sizes in the entire audit — Pashler et al.'s **494% increase in final retention** from supplying the correct answer after an incorrect response — and the system currently does nothing at exactly this moment beyond recording the failure.

**Honorable mentions just below the cutoff** (higher effort or narrower impact, but still worth doing): Q6's lapse partial-retention rule (real impact, but requires reworking the lapse-interval formula — medium effort); Q5's conversion of the roadblock gate to genuine cross-session successive relearning (meaningful impact on EF-trajectory integrity, but requires session-boundary state tracking — medium-high effort); Q7's leech-threshold adjustment (trivial effort, but narrower impact than the top 5); Q11's tier-smoothing (medium effort, refines an already-directionally-sound mechanism rather than fixing a contradicted one); Q1's full FSRS migration (the highest absolute-impact item in the whole audit, but by far the highest effort, requiring a data-backfill and optimizer pipeline — appropriate as a larger follow-on project, not a quick win).
